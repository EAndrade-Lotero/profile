## Division of linguistic labor

How do environmental affordances, spatial arrangements, repeated experiences, reward structures, and the observed behavior of others are instrumental in the construction of interaction patterns individuals come to represent? An important step toward addressing this question is to recognize that the social environment includes opportunities to interact with other humans in complementary ways. The human environment is constituted not only by the physical world but also, fundamentally, by other people. An environment in which individuals possess different skills and knowledge enables them to achieve goals mediated by their linguistic interaction. We argue that uttering words whose meaning is deferred to experts constitutes a form of cognitive offloading, whereby individuals rely on other people to reduce the mental effort required to perform cognitive tasks.

To investigate this possibility empirically, we conducted an experiment in which pairs of participants first acquired complementary expertise, and then they could use technical terms to exploit each others skills \citep{AndradeETAL:22,PhiLang:19}. In the task, participants learned to discriminate between subclasses of highly confusable dog breeds, with each participant specializing in a different subclass. They then completed a classification task that they could solve independently or by querying their partner. The results showed that participants' querying rates became aligned, that more frequent querying was associated with better classification performance, and that participants in the expert role rated themselves as more expert than participants in a baseline condition in which they worked alone.
